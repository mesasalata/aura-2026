<Section id="overview" title="Hardware overview" kicker="01">

AURA’s physical layer is an **inline siphon** into a small sampling chamber, a **FET** whose gate carries the DNA catapult, and **reference electrodes** upstream of that chamber. The tool is meant to integrate into milking machines during construction, with minimal upkeep costs and expertise.

</Section>

<Section id="siphon" title="Siphon & chamber" kicker="02">

During each milking a small sample of collected milk is siphoned into a chamber so the fluid passes over the biosensor surface. Monitoring is consistent - each cow, many times a day - which is how we get to the earliest possible flag.

**Design choices.**
- Lives in the milking line, not a separate lab errand
- Small volume, short path, so the catapult sees fresh sample
- Built in at construction rather than bolted on as extra ritual

<Section id="fet" title="FET surface" kicker="03">

The field-effect transistor is the reader. DNA catapults sit on the gate; when miR-223 opens a catapult, charge moves away from the surface and current between source and drain ticks. Electrical-field changes are the output - no colour card, no fluorescence bench.

<Section id="electrodes" title="Reference electrodes" kicker="04">

Electrodes sit **before** the sampling chamber and register ionic concentration and electrochemical activity. That baseline calibrates the FET residual so highly ionic milk is less likely to raise a false flag.

</Section>

<Section id="integration" title="In the parlour" kicker="05">

Hardware followed stakeholder input: sit in the line, cost less than the loss it prevents, return a readout before the tank is mixed. The parlour computer (not a laptop at the stall) holds the score and, when it stays high, a quiet alert on the farmer’s phone or the parlour display.

The same siphon-and-FET idea can be retargeted to other bacterial strands or biomarkers for cheaper, more specific diagnostics.

</Section>
