<Section id="overview" title="Overview" kicker="01">

AURA ultimately aims to detect miRNA-223 using a field-effect transistor (FET). Before testing an electrical readout, however, we first needed a reliable way to measure whether our DNA catapult probe was behaving as intended.

We therefore used **fluorescence as a proxy for probe opening**. This allowed us to:

- compare different probe designs;
- measure target binding;
- test whether the probe still worked at 39 °C;
- measure signal reduction after RNase H treatment;
- and test whether the probe could respond to a new target after resetting.

Our main measurements were **relative fluorescence units (RFU)**, **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)**, percentage fluorescence change, and standard deviation between technical replicates.

</Section>

<Section id="principle" title="Measurement principle" kicker="02">

Our catapult probe contains **BHQ-1 at the 5′ end** and **FAM at the 3′ end**.

In the closed state, FAM and BHQ-1 are close together, so BHQ-1 quenches the FAM fluorescence. When a complementary target binds, the probe opens and separates the fluorophore from the quencher, causing fluorescence to increase.

- **Closed probe:** low fluorescence
- **Target-bound probe:** higher fluorescence

We therefore used fluorescence as an indirect measurement of the probe’s conformational state.

<Section id="quantities" title="What we measured" kicker="03">

### Relative fluorescence units

The plate reader reports fluorescence as **relative fluorescence units (RFU)**.

RFU is an instrument-dependent measurement of fluorescence intensity. We did not create a fluorescence standard curve, so RFU could not be converted into an absolute number of fluorophore molecules or opened probes. Instead, RFU was used to compare samples measured under the same experimental conditions.

### Signal-to-noise ratio

During probe optimization, high target fluorescence alone was not enough. A useful sensor also needed low fluorescence when no target was present.

We therefore calculated the **signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)** as the mean fluorescence with target divided by the mean fluorescence without target:

<Callout variant="lab" title="SNR">
  <div className="text-base text-ink">SNR = mean RFU with target / mean RFU without target</div>
</Callout>

On this page, SNR specifically refers to this target-to-no-target fluorescence ratio. A higher SNR means that the target-positive and target-negative states are more clearly separated.

</Section>

Measurements were performed in **Greiner Bio-One 384-well PS µCLEAR®, black, non-binding microplates (Item 781906).**

For the main assays, reactions had a final volume of **40 µL per well**, with approximately **100 nM probe**, **100 nM target** where applicable, **1× PBS** and **5 mM MgCl₂**. Each main condition was measured in **three technical replicates**.

<Section id="snr" title="Using SNR to select our probe" kicker="05">

Our first measurement goal was to compare different catapult probe designs.

Rather than selecting the probe with the highest absolute fluorescence, we wanted the design with the clearest difference between its no-target and target-bound states.

We compared four miRNA-223 designs and also included the original **miR-21 catapult design** that inspired our sensor. The miR-21 probe was based on the catapult-like DNA actuator described by Park *et al.*; the SNR shown below was measured in **our own fluorescence assay**, not taken from the paper.

Fluorescence was measured at **30 °C for 120 minutes**, with measurements every 5 minutes. At 120 minutes:

The **3′-end-2-bp-shortened miRNA-223 probe had the highest SNR, 17.96**, and was therefore selected for further testing.

Importantly, it did not have the highest absolute target fluorescence. Its high SNR resulted from a strong target signal combined with particularly low background fluorescence. This showed why SNR was a useful measurement for probe selection.

<Section id="replicates" title="Technical replicates and error bars" kicker="08">

Each main condition was measured using **three technical replicates**. For each time point, we calculated the mean fluorescence of the three wells. Variation between wells was represented using **population standard deviation (`STDEV.P`)**.

Graphs are therefore shown as **mean ± SD, n = 3 technical replicates**. These replicates measure variation between wells within the same experimental run.

Although experiments were carried out during two separate lab visits, the second visit tested different questions rather than independently repeating the first experiment. We therefore do not claim independent experimental replication.

</Section>

<Section id="rnaseh" title="Measuring RNase H-mediated signal reduction" kicker="10">

After confirming that the probe still responded at 39 °C, we tested whether its RNA-associated fluorescence could be reduced.

We used **Thermo Scientific RNase H (EN0201)** at **5 U/µL**. RNA-containing wells received **0.4 µL RNase H**, corresponding to approximately **2 U per well**.

RNase H was added near the top of the well, the plate was centrifuged to mix the reaction, and fluorescence measurement began immediately afterward. The delay between RNase H addition and the first measurement was approximately **1 minute**. Fluorescence was then measured every 5 minutes for 30 minutes at 39 °C. For the selected probe:

<DataTable
  head={["Condition", "0 min RFU", "30 min RFU", "% change"]}
  rows={[
    [<strong>miRNA-223 + RNase H</strong>, "26,466", "7,279", <strong>−72.5%</strong>],
    ["ssDNA reference", "37,469", "30,681", "−18.1%"],
    ["No-target control", "2,441", "2,651", "+8.6%"],
  ]}
/>

The miRNA-223 + RNase H condition showed a strong, progressive decrease in fluorescence. After 30 minutes, only approximately **27.5% of the initial fluorescence remained**. The ssDNA reference showed a much smaller decrease, while the no-target control remained low.

<WikiFigure src="/images/Measurement%20RNase%20H%20reduction.png" caption="RNase H-associated fluorescence reduction: miRNA-223 + RNase H, ssDNA reference and no-target conditions over 30 minutes at 39 °C." width="lg" />

### Interpreting this result

The ssDNA reference also decreased by approximately 18.1%, showing that some fluorescence loss occurred independently of RNA degradation. Possible causes include temperature equilibration, photobleaching, changes in probe structure or general measurement variation.

The ssDNA reference was also **not treated with RNase H**, so this experiment does not prove absolute RNase H specificity. We therefore interpret the result as **consistent with RNase H-mediated degradation of the RNA target contributing strongly to the fluorescence decrease**.

</Section>

<Section id="redetection" title="Measuring whether the probe could respond again" kicker="11">

Reducing fluorescence alone does not demonstrate that a sensor can be reused. The probe also needs to remain capable of detecting another target.

After RNase H treatment, fresh **ssDNA miR-223** was added to the previously treated samples. All four probe designs showed renewed increases in fluorescence. For the selected 3′-end-2-bp-shortened probe, fluorescence rose from **24,807 RFU to 50,117 RFU** over 30 minutes, an increase of approximately **102%**.

<WikiFigure src="/images/Measurement%20ssDNA%20re-addition.png" caption="Previously RNase H-treated probes produced renewed fluorescence after fresh complementary ssDNA miR-223 was added." width="lg" />

This supports **proof-of-concept reuse at the molecular fluorescence level**.

However, the second target was ssDNA rather than another RNA target, and only one reset/re-detection cycle was tested. We therefore do not claim repeated multi-cycle RNA sensing.

</Section>

<Section id="protocol" title="Protocol for future teams" kicker="14">

A similar fluorescence workflow could be used for other fluorophore-quencher nucleic-acid sensors.

1. Prepare probe and target/control solutions in the chosen buffer.
2. For our assay, use **1× PBS with 5 mM MgCl₂**.
3. Prepare reactions containing approximately **100 nM probe and 100 nM target**, where applicable.
4. Use a final volume of **40 µL per well**.
5. Prepare at least three technical replicates.
6. Include a no-target control, complementary positive target and background blank.
7. Measure FAM fluorescence using consistent excitation, emission and gain settings.
8. Maintain a constant temperature during the assay.
9. Record fluorescence at fixed time intervals.
10. Keep individual replicate values before calculating the mean.
11. Calculate mean RFU, SD, SNR and fluorescence change as required.
12. Compare target-containing samples with matched controls rather than interpreting RFU alone.
13. Report the instrument settings and whether any blank subtraction or calibration was performed.

</Section>

<Section id="limitations" title="Limitations" kicker="15">

Our measurement system has several important limitations.

First, we used **technical replicates rather than independent experimental repeats**. Our error bars therefore describe variation between wells within one run.

Second, some experiments used an **ssDNA analogue of miRNA-223**. DNA was useful for controlled probe testing, but DNA and RNA do not necessarily behave identically.

Third, these measurements were performed in **buffer rather than real milk**. Milk could affect fluorescence and probe behaviour through proteins, lipids, salts, nucleases or autofluorescence.

Finally, fluorescence measures the molecular behaviour of the probe, not the electrical response of our proposed FET sensor. Testing the FET readout is therefore an important future step.

</Section>

<Section id="beyond" title="Why this approach is useful beyond AURA" kicker="16">

This workflow could also be useful for teams developing **molecular beacons, hairpin probes or other fluorophore-quencher nucleic-acid sensors**. These systems face similar questions:

- Is the background signal low?
- Does target binding produce a clear signal?
- Which design gives the best target-to-background separation?
- How quickly does the signal change?
- Does the sensor still work at different temperatures?
- Can the signal be reduced or reset?
- Can the probe respond again afterward?

Combining **RFU, SNR, time-course measurements, controls and replicate variability** provides a simple way to answer these questions quantitatively.

For AURA, this approach allowed us to measure the probe’s **OFF state, ON state, target discrimination, response at 39 °C, signal reduction and proof-of-concept reuse** before moving toward FET-based detection.

</Section>

<Section id="conclusion" title="Conclusion" kicker="17">

Fluorescence gave us a quantitative way to measure the behaviour of our DNA catapult probe.

Using SNR, we selected the **3′-end-2-bp-shortened miRNA-223 design**, which produced the highest SNR of the tested miRNA-223 probes.

The selected probe remained responsive to both complementary ssDNA and synthetic miRNA-223 at **39 °C**.

RNase H treatment then reduced RNA-associated fluorescence by approximately **72.5%**, and the probe produced a renewed fluorescence response when fresh complementary target was added.

Together, these measurements provided a quantitative framework for evaluating the molecular sensing and reset behaviour of AURA before integration with an electrical FET readout.

</Section>
