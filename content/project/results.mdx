<Section id="overview" title="Results overview" kicker="01">

This year, we adapted the DNA catapult to recognise miR-223, optimised the probe through several engineering cycles, demonstrated detection of synthetic miR-223 RNA at 39 °C, and investigated methods for resetting the sensor.

All of our wet-lab results come from a fluorescence assay. We used **fluorescence as a proxy for probe opening**: the catapult probe carries BHQ-1 at the 5′ end and FAM at the 3′ end, so when a complementary target binds and opens the probe, fluorescence increases. The full methods are on our Experiments and Measurement pages, and the design reasoning behind each step is on our Engineering page.

<Callout title="What these results do not yet show">

Fluorescence measures the molecular behaviour of the probe, not the electrical response of our proposed FET sensor. The measurements were performed in buffer rather than real milk, and the hardware was designed but not constructed. Direct FET-based detection, testing in raw milk, and integration of the complete system into the milking workflow are the next stages.

</Callout>

</Section>

These results suggested that replacing the miR-21 recognition region with a sequence complementary to miR-223 preserved the intended catapult structure and allowed formation of a stable target-bound complex. This was computational evidence for the feasibility of the adapted design, not experimental confirmation.

<Section id="selection" title="A shortened probe gave the clearest signal" kicker="03">

Four miR-223 probe designs, plus the original miR-21 catapult, were exposed to a synthetic DNA analogue of their target and monitored for 120 minutes at 30 °C. Probe performance was compared using the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR): mean fluorescence with target divided by mean fluorescence without target.

<DataTable
  head={["Probe design", "SNR"]}
  rows={[
    ["Original miR-21 catapult", "6.41"],
    ["miR-223 baseline (v2)", "6.73"],
    ["miR-223 stem −2 bp", "11.81"],
    ["miR-223 1 bp invasion-region replacement", "15.59"],
    [<strong>miR-223 3′ end −2 bp</strong>, <strong>17.96</strong>],
  ]}
/>

The 3′-end-2-bp-shortened probe had the highest SNR, approximately **2.7-fold higher** than the baseline miR-223 design, and was selected for further testing. It did not have the highest absolute target fluorescence: its high SNR came from a strong target signal combined with particularly low background fluorescence.

<WikiFigure src="/images/Experiments%20water%20control%20vs%20miR-223.png" caption="Mean fluorescence after 120 minutes: water control vs. miR-223 detection." width="md" />

The water control exhibited minimal fluorescence (208 RFU), while target-containing samples produced substantially higher signals (19,135–22,130 RFU).

</Section>

<Section id="temperature" title="miR-223 RNA opens the probe at 39 °C" kicker="04">

Milk leaving the udder is warm, and higher temperature can weaken hybridization. We therefore tested the selected probe at 39 °C with no target, a complementary ssDNA analogue, and synthetic miR-223 RNA, over 30 minutes.

<Section id="reset" title="RNase H reduces the signal, and the probe responds again" kicker="05">

For the selected probe, RNase H treatment of miRNA-223-bound samples reduced fluorescence from approximately 26,466 RFU to 7,279 RFU over 30 minutes at 39 °C, a **72.5% decrease**. The ssDNA reference decreased by 18.1%, and the no-target control stayed low.

<WikiFigure src="/images/Experiments%20fluorescence%20remaining%20after%20RNase%20H.png" caption="Fluorescence remaining after 30 minutes during the RNase H experiment. RNA + RNase H samples retained only approximately 27–35% of their initial fluorescence across all four probe designs, whereas DNA reference samples retained approximately 75–85%." width="lg" />

The pattern held across all four probe designs. The signal did not return fully to the no-target level (7,279 RFU against 2,651 RFU), so the sensor was not completely regenerated.

When fresh ssDNA miR-223 was then added, all four designs showed renewed fluorescence; the selected probe rose from approximately 24,807 RFU to 50,117 RFU (about **102%**). This is proof-of-concept evidence for **signal reduction followed by renewed target detection** at the molecular fluorescence level.

Two limits apply. The DNA reference was not treated with RNase H, so the result is consistent with, but does not prove, RNase H-specific degradation of the RNA target. And the second detection used ssDNA rather than RNA, with only one reset/re-detection cycle tested, so we do not claim repeated multi-cycle RNA sensing.

</Section>

<Section id="beads" title="Bead-based strand displacement was inconclusive" kicker="06">

A second reset strategy used biotinylated 9 bp and 13 bp replacer strands, magnetic streptavidin beads, and a sensorResetter strand. After bead processing, all four target/replacer conditions stayed at low fluorescence (roughly 300–680 RFU) with no clear time-dependent change.

Because many beads were likely lost during washing, the low final fluorescence could reflect either successful resetting or physical loss of bead-bound sensor. The first wash eluate was much less fluorescent than no-bead controls, which suggests the sensor complexes did initially attach to the beads. Improving bead retention and washing is needed before this reset strategy can be evaluated reliably.

</Section>
