<Section id="udder" title="The Udder as a System" kicker="01">

The mammary gland is responsible for producing milk, but it is also vulnerable to infection. Milk is produced in small structures called alveoli before flowing through ducts towards the teat. The teat canal acts as an important barrier against bacteria entering the udder.

When pathogens manage to pass this barrier, the cow's immune system responds by sending white blood cells to the affected area. This immune response causes inflammation, which is a key feature of mastitis.

**Why this matters for AURA.** Understanding how the immune system responds to infection helped us consider which biological changes could serve as useful signals. Rather than focusing only on the bacteria themselves, we became interested in detecting signs of the cow's response to infection.

</Section>

<Section id="causes" title="What Causes Mastitis?" kicker="02">

Mastitis can be caused by different microorganisms, which are often grouped according to how they spread.

### Contagious pathogens

Organisms such as *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus agalactiae* can spread between cows, particularly during milking.

### Environmental pathogens

Bacteria such as *E. coli*, *Streptococcus uberis*, and *Klebsiella* can enter the udder from the surrounding environment, including bedding, soil, and manure.

### Opportunistic pathogens

Coagulase-negative staphylococci are commonly found on the skin and around the teat. Although infections caused by these bacteria are often milder, some can persist and affect udder health.

**Why this matters for AURA.** One of the challenges we identified is the variety of microorganisms that can cause mastitis. A detection method targeting only one pathogen may not be suitable for every case. This led us to explore host-response biomarkers associated with inflammation, which could provide a broader indication of changes occurring in the udder. However, these signals would still need to be validated to establish how reliably they indicate mastitis.

</Section>

<Section id="scc" title="Understanding Somatic Cell Count" kicker="03">

The somatic cell count (SCC) measures the number of cells present in milk, including white blood cells involved in the immune response. When inflammation develops in the udder, the SCC generally increases as more immune cells enter the milk.

SCC is already widely used in dairy farming to monitor udder health and milk quality. It therefore provides an important reference point for understanding how biological changes can be translated into measurable information.

**Why this matters for AURA.** Looking at SCC helped us understand the importance of producing a signal that is easy to interpret and relevant to farmers. It also highlighted the challenge of developing a method that could complement existing monitoring approaches rather than simply repeat what they already provide.

</Section>

<Section id="biomarkers" title="Biomarkers: Signals of Inflammation" kicker="04">

When a cow develops mastitis, the composition of its milk can change. Alongside changes in SCC, inflammation can affect the levels of certain enzymes, proteins, and other biological molecules.

These molecules, known as biomarkers, can provide information about processes taking place inside the body. Host-response biomarkers are particularly interesting because they reflect the cow's response to inflammation rather than the presence of one specific microorganism.

**Why this matters for AURA.** Exploring these biomarkers is an important step in deciding what our biosensor could detect. However, not every biomarker is equally suitable. We need to consider how early its levels change, how reliably it reflects inflammation, and whether it can be measured using a practical and accessible system. Identifying and validating a suitable marker is therefore a key part of AURA's engineering process.

</Section>

<Section id="economics" title="The Economic Impact of Mastitis" kicker="05">

Mastitis is not only an animal health issue; it also creates significant economic challenges for dairy farmers. Infected cows may produce less milk, milk quality can be affected, and farmers may face additional costs associated with veterinary care and treatment.
