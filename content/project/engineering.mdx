<Section id="design-1" title="Design 1" kicker="01">

The catapult probe developed by Park *et al.* was originally designed to detect **miR-21**. Binding of the target miRNA causes a conformational change in the DNA structure, which can alter the surface potential detected by a field-effect transistor (FET). 1-s2.0-S1385894725006059-main

For this project, the target was **miR-223**, a biomarker being investigated for mastitis detection. The original miR-21 recognition region therefore had to be adapted to recognize miR-223 while maintaining the overall catapult structure.

The sequences of miR-21 and miR-223 were compared, and the target-recognition region of the original catapult was replaced with a sequence complementary to miR-223. Two initial miR-223 designs were produced, including one designed to preserve the geometry of the original catapult as closely as possible.

<Section id="learn-1" title="Learn 1" kicker="04">

The RNAfold predictions suggested that the miR-21 recognition region could be replaced with a miR-223 recognition sequence **without disrupting the intended catapult structure**. A complete redesign of the probe was therefore unnecessary.

However, the next objective was not only to detect miR-223, but eventually to **remove the bound target so that the sensor could be reset and reused**. Strong base-pairing between miR-223 and the probe could make this removal more difficult.

The next design cycle therefore focused on **modifying selected base-paired regions of the probe**. The aim was to make the target easier to remove while maintaining the structural change required for detection.

<Section id="design-2" title="Design 2" kicker="05">

Following Cycle 1, the adapted miR-223 catapult was predicted to retain the intended stem-loop structure. The next engineering goal was to make the sensor **reusable**.

After detection, miR-223 would need to be removed from the catapult so that the probe could return to its original state. Strong base-pairing between the target and probe could make this removal difficult. Several variants were therefore designed with changes to selected base-paired regions, with the aim of making future target removal easier **without preventing the probe from responding to miR-223**.

The variants selected for experimental testing were:

- the baseline **miR223CatapultDesign_v2**;
- a probe with **1 bp replaced in the invasion region**;
- a probe with the **stem shortened by 2 bp**; and
- a probe with the **3′ end shortened by 2 bp**.

<Section id="build-2" title="Build 2" kicker="06">

To experimentally compare the designs, the probes were synthesized with a **BHQ-1 quencher** and **6-FAM fluorophore** positioned at opposite ends of the catapult sequence.

In the closed state, the fluorophore and quencher remain close together, producing low fluorescence. Opening of the probe separates them, allowing fluorescence to increase. This provided a simple way to test whether the modified catapult designs could still respond to the target before integration with the FET.

A synthetic **DNA analogue of miR-223** was used as the target in this first experimental comparison to reduce experimental complexity. The catapult probes were tested at a final concentration of **100 nM** in **1× PBS supplemented with 5 mM MgCl₂**, with fluorescence monitored for **120 minutes at 30.3°C**.

The results demonstrated that reducing or altering base-pairing in selected regions did **not prevent the catapult from responding to the target**. In fact, several modified designs produced a stronger signal relative to background than the baseline probe.

Importantly, this experiment tested **detection performance**, not target removal itself. Whether the shortened design actually makes miR-223 easier to remove was therefore left for a later engineering cycle.

</Section>

<Section id="learn-2" title="Learn 2" kicker="08">

The experiment showed that modifications intended to support future sensor regeneration could be introduced **without losing the catapult's ability to respond to the target**.

Among the tested variants, the **3′-end 2-bp-shorter design** produced the strongest signal relative to background and was therefore selected as the lead architecture for continued development.

This result was particularly useful because shortening the probe had initially been introduced to make future target removal more feasible, yet the modification also maintained - and under these assay conditions improved - the measurable fluorescence response.

However, two major questions remained. The experiment had used a **synthetic DNA analogue rather than actual miR-223 RNA**, and the assay conditions did not yet represent the intended operating conditions of the final milk sensor.

The next engineering cycle therefore carried the **3′-end 2-bp-shorter probe** forward to determine whether it could function with actual miR-223 and under conditions closer to those expected during mastitis testing.
