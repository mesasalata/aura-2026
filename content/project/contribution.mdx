<Section id="overview" title="Contributions" kicker="01">

Through AURA, we developed and characterized miR-223-responsive DNA catapult probes, investigated methods for resetting the sensor, and created open-source software for interpreting biosensor measurements. By documenting both successful and unsuccessful approaches, we hope our work can support future iGEM teams developing reusable nucleic-acid biosensors.

</Section>

<Section id="probes" title="Engineered miR-223-responsive DNA probes" kicker="02">

A central contribution of AURA was adapting the DNA catapult sensing architecture to recognize miR-223, a microRNA associated with the inflammatory response to mastitis.

We first designed a baseline miR-223-responsive probe and then created several variants to investigate how changes to the probe structure affected its response. The probes were designed with 5′ BHQ-1 and 3′ 6-FAM, so that opening of the hairpin separates the quencher and fluorophore and produces an increase in fluorescence.

<Section id="characterization" title="Characterizing catapult performance" kicker="03">

We developed a fluorescence-based workflow to compare the different catapult designs under the same conditions. Their signal-to-noise ratios were:

- baseline probe: **6.73**
- stem-shortened probe: **11.81**
- 1 bp invasion-region replacement: **15.59**
- 3′-end-shortened probe: **17.96**

The 3′-end-shortened probe, BBa_26U24IU9, produced the strongest response and was therefore selected for later experiments.

This comparison also demonstrates how relatively small changes to probe architecture can substantially alter sensor performance. Future teams adapting similar probes could use the same iterative approach to design, test, and compare variants.

</Section>

<Section id="rna" title="Validation with miR-223 RNA" kicker="04">

After initially characterizing the system using a complementary DNA analogue, we tested our selected probe with synthetic miR-223 RNA at 39 °C.

The probe showed a substantial increase in fluorescence in the presence of the RNA target, demonstrating that the adapted catapult could respond to miR-223 RNA rather than only to a DNA analogue.

Our experiments were performed with synthetic targets rather than raw milk, so further work is still required to characterize the probe in complex biological samples. Nevertheless, these results provide a starting point for teams interested in adapting the catapult architecture for microRNA detection.

</Section>

<Section id="reset" title="Exploring sensor reset and reuse" kicker="05">

Because AURA is ultimately intended to function as a reusable sensor, we also investigated methods for removing bound target and restoring the probe.

### RNase H reset

We tested RNase H, which can degrade RNA within an RNA–DNA hybrid. For our selected 3′-end-shortened probe, fluorescence decreased by approximately 72.5% following RNase H treatment. Addition of fresh target afterwards produced a renewed fluorescence response, showing that the probe remained functional after the reset process.

These results are consistent with RNase H-mediated removal of bound RNA. However, our DNA reference was not treated with RNase H under identical conditions, so additional controls would be required to determine how much of the fluorescence decrease resulted specifically from enzymatic RNA degradation.

Documenting this limitation provides future teams with a clearer starting point for improving enzymatic sensor regeneration.

### Strand-displacement reset

We also investigated a second reset strategy using short biotinylated replacer strands, streptavidin beads, and a separate sensorResetter strand.

<Section id="software" title="UdderNet software" kicker="06">

Our contribution also extended beyond the molecular sensor.

We developed UdderNet, an open-source software platform designed to receive biosensor and cow-level data, process the measurements, explain how a screening result was produced, generate reports, and alert farm staff when further attention may be required.

UdderNet includes a documented API, contextual machine-learning analysis, transparent presentation of BioFET measurements and thresholds, cow-specific PDF reports, real-time and background phone alerts, and automated testing.

The software is released under the MIT License, allowing future teams to adapt the platform for other biosensors or monitoring applications.

</Section>

<Section id="future" title="What future teams can build on" kicker="07">

Future teams could build on AURA by adapting the catapult recognition sequence to new nucleic-acid targets, further optimizing our probe variants, applying our fluorescence-characterization workflow, improving reset strategies, or testing the selected probe on an immobilized sensing surface.

</Section>
