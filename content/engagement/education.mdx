<Section id="overview" title="Education" kicker="01">

Our education work aimed to make synthetic biology approachable to people with very different levels of scientific experience. Across a weekly club, workshops, community talks, an educational website, a card game, and written science communication, we adapted both the content and format of our teaching to different audiences and used their responses to improve how we communicated complex ideas.

</Section>

<Section id="approach" title="Our Approach" kicker="02">

Rather than using one activity for every audience, we developed several ways for people to engage with synthetic biology. Younger students learned through physical models and games, older students explored applications and experimental design, and adults were introduced to our project through simplified explanations connecting biology with electronic sensing.

Across these activities, we focused on three principles: making technical ideas understandable without removing their scientific meaning, encouraging participation rather than one-way presentation, and creating educational resources that could continue to be used beyond a single event.

</Section>

<Section id="website" title="An Accessible Synthetic Biology Website" kicker="03">

<WikiFigure

To make synthetic biology more accessible, we developed an educational website that provides a central space for anyone to learn about synthetic biology, regardless of their prior knowledge. The platform is designed to support self-paced learning while accommodating different learning needs through an inclusive and accessible design.

The website consists of three main sections. The “Learn” section contains structured learning modules that explain synthetic biology concepts using concise text, visual aids, and interactive activities. Complex topics are divided into smaller sections, allowing users to build their understanding step by step. The “Explore the World” section connects synthetic biology to real-world applications through curated articles covering current developments, ethics, and biotechnology. The “Our Project” section documents our team’s progress, milestones, and project updates throughout the iGEM season.

Accessibility and inclusivity were central considerations throughout the design process. The website was designed to be accessible to users of different ages and individuals with diverse learning needs, including dyslexia and ADHD. Scientific terms are accompanied by hover-over definitions, allowing readers to quickly understand unfamiliar terminology without disrupting the reading flow. To accommodate different learning preferences and time constraints, users can choose between structured learning modules, interactive activities, articles, or our “SynBio in 3 Minutes” newsletter, which explains one synthetic biology concept in a concise format that can be read in just a few minutes.

### Our platform in practice

<WikiFigure
  src="/images/Education%20website%20lessons.jpg"
  width="lg"
  caption="Introductory lessons and real-world stories on the website homepage."
/>

The completed platform includes structured learning modules at different difficulty levels, real-world synthetic biology stories, interactive learning features, and accessibility tools. The homepage currently features four introductory learning modules and four real-world application stories, with additional content accessible through the wider platform.

After receiving feedback from students and teachers at our school, we identified several ways to improve the website’s accessibility and user engagement. This feedback reinforced the importance of breaking complex topics into shorter sections, providing clearer explanations of scientific terminology, and offering different ways for users to engage with the material.

It also informed ideas for future development, including an AI-assisted learning tool that could adapt explanations to a user’s interests and level of understanding, as well as a moderated Communication Hub for sharing educational resources. We also plan to continue expanding the learning modules, interactive activities, and accessibility features as we collect feedback from a wider range of users.

<Section id="game" title="DNA: Unlocking Synthetic Biology Game" kicker="04">

DNA: Unlocking Synthetic Biology is an educational card game designed to make synthetic biology concepts interactive and accessible. Through gameplay, players encounter ideas including plasmid design, promoters, ribosome-binding sites, terminators, standardized biological parts, and assembly methods such as Gibson and Golden Gate assembly.

The core gameplay and rules were developed during our previous iGEM season and remained unchanged this year. This year, we focused on using the game as an educational resource. We played and tested the game among ourselves and with other students, including classmates and friends. These sessions allowed us to observe how players interacted with the rules and whether the biological concepts could be understood through gameplay. Informal questions and feedback helped us identify areas where instructions or concepts needed clearer explanation and gave us a practical way to evaluate the game as an educational tool. Based on player feedback, we redesigned the game cards to be more distinctive and visually appealing.

We also wanted the game to reach people beyond our immediate school community. Through a Kickstarter campaign, we raised approximately CHF 1,700 to support physical production and distribution. We are currently using these funds to print 15 copies for backers who contributed CHF 20 or more, produce stickers for supporters who contributed CHF 5 or more, and cover shipping so that backers can receive the materials without additional cost.

By turning synthetic biology concepts into a fun game, we aimed to create an educational resource that could continue to be used beyond a single workshop or presentation.

</Section>

<Section id="rolle" title="Community Outreach in the Rolle Region" kicker="05">

During a visit to our school, we presented AURA to more than 40 adults from the Rolle region. Because we could not assume prior biological knowledge, we began with the problem of mastitis and simplified the biology behind the sensor. The questions from the audience showed us that transition from molecular recognition to electronic detection was the most difficult part of the system to communicate. We used this experience to make later presentations more step-by-step and visual, introducing the practical problem before adding technical details.

</Section>

<Section id="club" title="Synthetic Biology Club" kicker="06">

To increase awareness of synthetic biology and provide students with opportunities to explore the field in greater depth, we founded the Synthetic Biology Club at Le Rosey. The club met weekly and combined interactive learning activities, including games, worksheets, and discussions, with lectures introducing fundamental concepts in synthetic biology. Throughout the year, members explored topics including genetic circuit engineering, CRISPR-based gene editing, and nanotechnology for targeted drug delivery. By combining theoretical content with hands-on educational activities, the club created an accessible introduction for beginners while allowing students with prior knowledge to further develop their understanding.

### What we learned and how we improved

Feedback from club members showed that students had different levels of prior knowledge and different interests within synthetic biology. In response, we began adapting sessions more carefully to the audience, balancing introductory explanations with more advanced topics as members developed their understanding.

The club also became a starting point for wider science communication. Building on the interests raised during meetings, we developed additional educational activities and contributed to a Reatch article aimed at introducing synthetic biology to a broader audience.

</Section>

<Section id="isl" title="International School of Lausanne Workshop" kicker="07">

We held a synthetic biology workshop for students at the International School of Lausanne (ISL).

<WikiFigure
  src="/images/Education%20ISL%20workshop.jpg"
  width="md"
  caption="Team members present the future of synthetic biology to ISL students."
/>

The session began with the relationship between DNA, genes, proteins, and traits before moving into how scientists can engineer biological systems using DNA constructs and plasmids. We then introduced a real-world application of engineered bacteria and used our educational card game to give students a more interactive way to apply what they had learned. The presentation itself covered synthetic biology fundamentals, plasmids, engineered DNA, and a case study on landmine-detecting bacteria.

### Feedback and What We Learned

The workshop was delivered in two separate classrooms and combined a presentation on synthetic biology with a Kahoot activity and an introduction to our educational website. During the sessions, several students took notes while we were presenting, providing a visible indication of engagement with the material. The teachers also responded positively to the presentation.

At the end of the session, we shared the link to our synthetic biology website so that students who were particularly interested could continue exploring the subject independently after the workshop.

From this experience, we learned that combining a presentation with an interactive activity and a resource students could return to afterwards gave us multiple ways to introduce the same topic rather than relying on a lecture alone, while also reinforcing the learning. The interested students now also have a permanent method to access synbio content through our website.

</Section>

<Section id="bookfete" title="Bookfête: Connecting Synthetic Biology to a Real-World Problem" kicker="09">

At Bookfête, four members of our team presented to around 40 students aged 13–17. We first introduced synthetic biology through examples in medicine, sustainability, and engineering, before explaining the Design–Build–Test–Learn cycle and the importance of responsible testing.

We then used AURA as a real-world case study, introducing mastitis, miR-223, DNA-probe recognition, and the idea of converting molecular binding into a FET signal. We also discussed the limitations and unanswered questions that remain before a complete sensor can be developed.

Student questions helped us identify which technical ideas needed clearer explanation and reinforced the value of linking synthetic biology to a concrete real-world problem.

</Section>

<Section id="reatch" title="Reatch: Science Communication Beyond Our School" kicker="10">

Our iGEM team also wrote an article titled “Could synthetic biology make organ transplants unnecessary?” to introduce synthetic biology through a medical question that readers could connect with. We explored different types of stem cells, how researchers grow organoids, and the challenges involved in developing functioning replacement organs. Alongside the potential benefits, we discussed scientific limitations and ethical questions surrounding the use of embryonic stem cells. We explained technical vocabulary throughout the article, aiming to make the subject understandable without losing scientific detail. The article was written for potential publication by Reatch, with the aim of reaching a wider audience and encouraging readers to consider both the possibilities and challenges of this developing field.

</Section>

<Section id="learned" title="What We Learned" kicker="12">

Across our activities, we learned that synthetic biology becomes easier to understand when it is connected to something concrete. Younger students engaged most with hands-on activities, while older students and adults responded better when technical ideas were linked to real-world applications.

We also found that questions and feedback were essential for improving our explanations. This led us to use more visual, step-by-step teaching and to create resources that could continue to be used after individual events.

</Section>
