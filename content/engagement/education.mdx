<Section id="overview" title="What we taught" kicker="01">

We set out to make synthetic biology easier to enter, at school and beyond it. This page records the Synthetic Biology Club, an educational website, a card game, a bake sale, a talk with younger students, Bookfête, the junior workshop on 6 October, and a workshop we are preparing for the International School of Lausanne.

</Section>

<Section id="club" title="Synthetic Biology Club" kicker="02">

To increase awareness of synthetic biology and provide students with opportunities to explore the field in greater depth, we founded the Synthetic Biology Club at Le Rosey. The club met weekly and combined interactive learning activities, including games, worksheets, and discussions, with lectures introducing fundamental concepts in synthetic biology. Throughout the year, members explored topics including genetic circuit engineering, CRISPR-based gene editing, and nanotechnology for targeted drug delivery. By combining theoretical content with hands-on educational activities, the club created an accessible introduction for beginners while allowing students with prior knowledge to further develop their understanding.

<Callout title="Feedback loop">

We plan to continue the Synthetic Biology Club next year to further expand students' engagement with synthetic biology. In collaboration with the club, we are also preparing an article with *Reach* to promote synthetic biology to a wider audience. Based on feedback from our members, we will develop a more structured programme tailored to their interests and prior knowledge. As members build a stronger foundation in synthetic biology, we also plan to introduce more advanced topics to support their continued learning.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="website" title="Educational website" kicker="03">

To make synthetic biology more accessible, we developed an educational website that provides a central space for anyone to learn about synthetic biology, regardless of their prior knowledge. The platform is designed to support self-paced learning while accommodating different learning needs through an inclusive and accessible design.

The website consists of three main sections. The Learn section contains structured learning modules that explain synthetic biology concepts using concise text, visual aids, and interactive activities. Complex topics are divided into smaller sections, allowing users to build their understanding step by step. The Explore the World section connects synthetic biology to real-world applications through curated articles covering current developments, ethics, and biotechnology. The Our Project section documents our team's progress, milestones, and project updates throughout the iGEM season.

Accessibility and inclusivity were central considerations throughout the design process. The website was designed to be accessible to users of different ages and individuals with diverse learning needs, including dyslexia and ADHD. Scientific terms are accompanied by hover-over definitions, allowing readers to quickly understand unfamiliar terminology without disrupting the reading flow. To accommodate different learning preferences and time constraints, users can choose between structured learning modules, interactive activities, articles, or our "SynBio in 3 Minutes" newsletter, which explains one synthetic biology concept in a concise format that can be read in just a few minutes.

<Callout title="Feedback loop">

Throughout the development of the website, we identified several features that could further improve accessibility and user engagement but were beyond the scope of this project. One planned addition is an AI-assisted learning tool that would personalise educational content based on a user's interests and level of understanding, while also providing context-specific explanations of scientific terminology. Another feature under consideration is a Communication Hub, where users could recommend educational resources to one another. To maintain a safe and focused learning environment, this would not function as an open chat but instead allow users to share resources through predefined prompts and recommendations.

As our website grows, we also plan to expand the range of learning modules, interactive activities, and accessibility features based on user feedback. Collecting feedback from students, educators, and members of the public will allow us to continuously refine the platform and ensure it remains an inclusive and effective resource for learning synthetic biology.

</Callout>

<Section id="isl" title="Workshop at ISL" kicker="04">

We plan to host a synthetic biology workshop for students at the International School of Lausanne (ISL) to introduce the fundamental principles of the field. The workshop will cover the relationship between DNA, genes, proteins, and traits before explaining how scientists engineer organisms using DNA constructs and plasmids. To demonstrate the real-world applications of synthetic biology, we will present the example of bacteria engineered to detect landmines. The workshop will conclude by introducing our educational synthetic biology game, allowing students to apply the concepts they have learned through a separate hands-on learning activity.

<Callout title="Feedback loop">

Following the workshop, we will collect feedback from participating students and teachers to evaluate both their understanding of synthetic biology and their overall experience. Students will be asked short questions before and after the workshop to assess how their knowledge and perception of synthetic biology have changed. We will also gather qualitative feedback on which parts of the workshop were most engaging, clear, or difficult to understand. This feedback will then be used to improve the structure, explanations, examples, and interactive elements of future workshops, creating a continuous cycle of testing, evaluation, and improvement.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="game" title="Card game" kicker="05">

With the goal of raising awareness about synthetic biology through an engaging learning experience, we developed an educational card game introducing players to fundamental concepts in synthetic biology. The game features custom illustrations inspired by our project, allowing players to engage with synthetic biology through a visually distinctive design. Through strategic gameplay, players become familiar with key concepts such as plasmids, genetic engineering, and experimental decision-making, encouraging them to think like synthetic biologists. By combining scientific accuracy with an enjoyable learning experience, we aim to make synthetic biology more accessible, spark curiosity about the field, and inspire players to further explore its real-world applications.

<Callout title="Feedback loop">

To improve the educational value and gameplay of our card game, we will test it with students and collect feedback on both their learning experience and enjoyment. Players will be asked whether the rules were clear, which concepts they learned, which parts were confusing, and how engaging they found the game. We will also observe how players interact with the cards and identify areas where scientific concepts or game mechanics may need clarification or adjustment. Based on this feedback, we will refine the rules, scientific content, card design, and level of difficulty before testing the improved version again. This iterative process will allow us to continuously improve the game while ensuring that it remains both scientifically accurate and enjoyable.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="juniors" title="Talk with the juniors" kicker="07">

To introduce our project to a wider and more diverse audience, we decided to involve the youngest members of our school community. We presented our project to nearly 50 students between the ages of 8 and 12, adapting our explanations to make the scientific concepts clear, engaging, and age-appropriate. We introduced them to the basic principles of biology and gradually connected these ideas to simple concepts in synthetic biology, helping them understand how scientists can work with DNA and biological systems. To make the session more memorable and interactive, we concluded with a hands-on activity in which students physically represented the structure of DNA. This allowed them to visualise the concept in a fun and accessible way while reinforcing what they had learned during the presentation.

<Callout title="Feedback loop">

To evaluate the effectiveness of the presentation, we will collect feedback from both the students and the teachers who attended the session. We will focus on whether the students understood the key biological concepts, which parts of the presentation they found most interesting, and whether the physical DNA activity helped them remember the information more clearly. We will also observe their participation and the types of questions they asked during the session. Based on this feedback, we can improve the way we explain complex scientific ideas to younger audiences, adjust the level of detail, and further develop interactive activities that make synthetic biology easier and more engaging to understand.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="bookfete" title="Bookfête" kicker="08">

During Bookfête, a school event where different authors and speakers are invited to celebrate books and educate students on a variety of topics, four members of our team initiated a talk to introduce our iGEM project, the iGEM competition, and synthetic biology. We spoke to around 40 students aged 13–17, introducing them to our project on mastitis and the science behind our detection method. We explained how our system uses a DNA probe and miRNA-223, which is complementary to the probe, and introduced the process through which the two bind, known as hybridization. We also explained what mastitis is, how it affects the udder of cows, and how infection can spread through contamination. Through this talk, we aimed to raise awareness about mastitis while demonstrating how synthetic biology can be applied to real-world challenges in animal health and agriculture.

<Callout title="Feedback loop">

Throughout the talk, we observed the students' engagement and the questions they asked to understand which aspects of our project were most accessible and interesting to them. Presenting concepts such as miRNA, DNA probes, and hybridization to students with different levels of biological knowledge also encouraged us to reflect on how we communicate complex scientific ideas. This experience showed us the importance of adapting our explanations to the audience and focusing on the core ideas and real-world relevance of our project. We can use these observations to improve future outreach by making our explanations clearer, more accessible, and more engaging for audiences without an extensive scientific background.

</Callout>

<Section id="workshop" title="Junior workshop" kicker="09">

On the **6th of October**, 3 members of our iGEM team hosted an interactive biology workshop for approximately **60 students in Classes 9–6 (Grades 3–6)**. The session lasted around **one hour** and was designed to introduce younger students to the basic ideas behind DNA and synthetic biology in a way that was accessible, engaging, and hands-on.

The workshop began with a short interactive presentation explaining what DNA is, what it looks like, its history, why it is important, and how complementary base pairing works. We also introduced the idea that understanding DNA is fundamental to synthetic biology, helping students see how basic biological concepts can connect to more advanced scientific applications later on.

To make the session more practical, students then worked in groups to build their own DNA models using rope and gummy candy. **Rope candies represented the sugar-phosphate backbones, while gummy bears represented the DNA bases.** The students were able to choose which gummy bears represented each base, but had to keep the complementary pairing consistent throughout their sequence. This allowed them to apply what they had just learned and make a model to show their understanding of the rules.

Most students did not know what synthetic biology was, and one of our main goals was to make synthetic biology feel **less intimidating and more approachable**. By introducing the basic concepts in a playful way, we hoped to give students a foundation that would make more complex biological ideas easier to understand in the future. The activity also encouraged participation and curiosity, with students asking questions throughout the presentation and while building their models.

Overall, the workshop was highly interactive, and the students remained engaged throughout the session. For our team, it was also a valuable opportunity to practice communicating scientific ideas clearly to a younger audience and to see how hands-on activities can make biology more memorable and accessible.
