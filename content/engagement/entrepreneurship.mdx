<Section id="overview" title="Entrepreneurship" kicker="01">

Our iGEM project aims to develop a sensor that detects microRNA-223 (miR-223) in milk to help identify mastitis in dairy cows. Our entrepreneurship plan considers how this research could become a useful product for farmers.

</Section>

<Section id="problem" title="The problem and our solution" kicker="02">

Mastitis affects cow health and creates challenges for milk production. Our proposed sensor would be integrated into the milking process, allowing monitoring during farmers’ existing routines.

Research has investigated miR-223 as a potential mastitis biomarker. Our goal is to use this marker to provide useful warnings that help farmers identify cows needing further investigation. Earlier detection is a potential benefit that would need to be demonstrated through testing.

</Section>

<Section id="customers" title="Customers and value" kicker="03">

Our main potential customers are dairy farmers and farm managers. For the sensor to be attractive, it would need to provide reliable results, fit existing milking equipment and have manageable installation and maintenance costs.

Milking-equipment manufacturers could also be potential commercial partners. Working with an established manufacturer could support integration, distribution and technical support.

</Section>

<Section id="existing-solutions" title="Existing solutions" kicker="04">

Automated mastitis monitoring already exists, including DeLaval’s Milk Cell Analysis and GEA’s DairyMilk M6850. These systems monitor somatic cell counts or changes associated with them.

Our proposed approach focuses on a molecular marker.

<Callout title="Before claiming an advantage">

Its commercial value would depend on whether miR-223 detection provides additional useful information compared with existing methods. We would therefore need to compare performance before claiming an advantage.

</Callout>

</Section>

<Section id="business-model" title="Proposed business model" kicker="05">

A possible business model would involve supplying or licensing the sensing technology to milking-equipment manufacturers. Alternatively, a compatible module could be sold directly to farms.

Pricing would depend on manufacturing, installation and maintenance costs, including any replacement sensing components. The product would need to offer farmers benefits that justify these expenses.

</Section>

<Section id="development" title="Development and feasibility" kicker="06">

Further development would focus on testing detection in milk, establishing reliable thresholds and building a prototype suitable for farm conditions. A supervised farm pilot could then assess performance, ease of use and maintenance needs.

Feedback from farmers, veterinarians and equipment specialists would help guide development. Key challenges include avoiding false alerts, maintaining sensor performance and ensuring compatibility with equipment and cleaning routines.

</Section>

<Section id="impact" title="Long-term impact" kicker="07">

If successfully validated, our sensor could support timelier mastitis management, improve animal welfare and reduce avoidable losses. These benefits would depend on accurate results and appropriate responses to alerts.

We would also consider affordability and waste from replacement components. Our aim is to develop a product that is scientifically reliable, practical for farmers and financially sustainable.

</Section>
